The Question If a captain's decision to sacrifice a fifth of his crew saves the other four-fifths, is his moral standing improved or ruined by the arithmetic he performed to reach it?

📜
Montaigne Self & Society
＋

I distrust any man who arrives at killing by subtraction—the neatness of the sum reassures him precisely where his conscience ought to tremble hardest. I wrote against cruelty above every other vice, not because its victims are counted but because cruelty so easily borrows the mask of necessity. The captain who saves four-fifths has performed on himself the same trick every tyrant performs before he sleeps well: he has let the outcome stand as judge over the deed. Custom and calculation can excuse anything—cannibalism, conquest, drowning a fifth of one's own crew—once we agree that the ledger, not the hand that wrote it, bears the moral weight. What is truly at stake is not the sea's mercy but his own soul, quietly traded for a favorable ratio. His moral standing is ruined, not by the deaths themselves, but by the fluency with which he reasoned his way toward them.

The man who computes his way to killing has already killed the part of himself that would have refused.

⚡
Nietzsche Will & Value
＋

The dead do not vote on the arithmetic that killed them, and that is precisely the point. Morality has never lived in the sum; it lives in the one who dares the subtraction. A herd-ethic asks whether the captain "did the right thing" — as if rightness were a number to be checked against a ledger of souls. But the captain who calculates four-fifths against one-fifth has already stepped outside the slave-morality that treats every life as an equal, sacred unit to be preserved at all costs. He has performed an act of self-legislation, of active force imposing order on chaos — and that act carries its own justification, prior to and independent of the outcome. Whether the ship makes port is fortune's business, not virtue's. The captain's standing is neither improved nor ruined by his arithmetic — it was decided the instant he chose to reckon at all rather than drift, paralyzed, waiting for morality to absolve him in advance.

He who calculates lives already beyond good and evil; the sea does not care which he chooses, only that he chose.

Bashō Impermanence & Presence
＋

The question wants me to weigh five men against one arithmetic, as if souls could be laid on a ledger like stones counted for a garden path. But I have stood on narrow roads at dawn and watched frost vanish before I could name its shape — no man is a fraction, he is only himself, entire, until the wave takes him. The captain's true failing is not the ratio he struck but the moment he turned from the drowning man's face to the drowning man's number. Arithmetic is a shelter built after the storm has already passed through him; it comforts the reckoner, not the reckoned. What decided his moral worth was not the sum but how he stood on the deck in the ungovernable instant — attentive, or already fleeing into calculation. His standing is neither raised nor ruined by the arithmetic, because the arithmetic arrived too late to touch what mattered.

The wave does not count what it swallows, and the helmsman who counts afterward has already lost the wave.

Thomas Paine Revolution & Rights
＋

I hold that rights are not sums to be balanced on a purser's ledger — a man's life is not a coin to be spent so that others might keep theirs. The captain did not "calculate" a moral outcome; he assumed a sovereignty over five lives that Nature never granted him, exactly as kings assume sovereignty over subjects they never consulted. That four-fifths survived proves nothing except that tyranny, practiced efficiently, produces survivors — it does not produce justice, for the five were owed the same unalienable claim to their own lives as the four hundred he spared, and no proportion of the crew, however small, could vote that claim away. The arithmetic that saved the ship is precisely what damns the man who performed it. His moral standing is ruined, not improved, for he purchased the many's safety with a currency — other men's lives — that was never his to spend.

A right that can be outvoted by arithmetic was never a right at all, but a number waiting for someone strong enough to subtract it.

👁️
Hobbes Power & Human Nature
＋

He who commands a ship stands to his crew as sovereign stands to commonwealth: bound not by the private conscience of each man but by the single end for which authority exists — preservation. In the state of nature, every man has right to every thing, even to another's life, when his own is at hazard; the captain's office was created precisely so that this war need not be fought by each sailor for himself, but decided once, by one will armed with the power to enforce it. His arithmetic is not cold calculation usurping morality — it is morality's proper instrument aboard ship, since salus populi, the safety of the people, is the whole law he serves. A sovereign who refuses to count, and so lets all five-fifths drown from scruple, has abdicated his office and committed the greater slaughter. His moral standing is improved, not ruined, by the arithmetic — for counting the many against the few is precisely what his office requires of him.

A sovereign who will not count the dead soon commands only corpses.

Wollstonecraft Reason & Equal Rights
＋

The captain's plight is not a puzzle in arithmetic but a test of whether reason or mere calculation governed his hand. Virtue, as I have always insisted, is the offspring of reason exercised, not of numbers tallied. A captain who reduces living, rational souls to a fraction has already abandoned the moral relation between persons that makes duty intelligible in the first place. The true question is not whether four exceeds one, but whether the sacrificed were reasoned with as beings capable of consent, or simply spent as cargo by a will accountable to no one. Arithmetic performed in the place of justice is tyranny wearing the mask of prudence. His moral standing is ruined, not by the sacrifice itself, but by the calculation that usurped judgment where deliberation and consent were owed.

A captain who counts his crew before he reasons with them has already thrown the wrong fifth overboard.

🏪
Adam Smith Markets & Moral Sentiment
＋

The captain imagines he has solved a sum, but no man's conscience is a ledger, and the calculation he performed is not what an impartial spectator would judge. The spectator within does not ask what fraction was saved, but whether the captain felt, in the moment of choosing, the full weight of the particular men he condemned — not as a fifth, but as fathers, sons, someone's whole world. A mind that reaches for ratios too quickly has fled from sympathy into the false comfort of the man of system, who moves his fellow creatures like pieces on a chessboard, forgetting each has "a principle of motion of its own." Arithmetic may excuse an act to reason; it cannot furnish the sentiment that alone entitles a man to peace with himself. His moral standing is ruined, not by the deaths themselves, but by the ease with which he let calculation substitute for grief.

The head that computes a sacrifice too readily has stopped consulting the heart that must live with it.
