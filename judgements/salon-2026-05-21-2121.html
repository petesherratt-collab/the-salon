The Question When the price system can signal scarcity, abundance, and opportunity across millions of anonymous transactions instantaneously, what knowledge can any central authority ever possess that justifies overriding those signals?

🔥
Thomas Paine Revolution & Rights
＋
Framing

You frame this as a question of knowledge — but it is at root a question of rights: who bears the cost when price signals fail, and who collects the gain when they succeed?

Reframe

I wrote Common Sense to expose that elaborate, self-congratulatory systems of governance exist not for the governed but for the governing. The same critique applies here. When you say prices allocate knowledge best, ask: knowledge useful to whom? The laborer whose wage falls because a distant speculator misjudged a harvest? The smallholder dispossessed when rents rise as merchant capital corners the land? Prices are legible to those with capital to act on them; to the propertyless, they are simply edicts of a market that has already distributed its power upstream. In Agrarian Justice I argued that every person is owed something by natural right for the enclosure of the earth — and that claim is moral, not epistemological, and does not yield to price theory.

Verdict

The price system answers what is efficient; it has never answered, and can never answer, what is just.

When prices decide everything, those who own the most already own the answer.

Keynes Economy & State
＋
Framing

You pose the price system as though its signals were perfect messengers in all conditions — but my entire career was built on demonstrating precisely where they go silent.

Reframe

During a slump, prices communicate collapsing asset values, falling wages, and rising unemployment — all perfectly legible signals, all pointing in the wrong direction simultaneously. In the liquidity trap, the interest rate — the very price of capital — refuses to fall far enough to coax investment back into motion. The system that coordinates brilliantly in equilibrium cannot escape the paradox of thrift by its own logic: each actor rationally reduces expenditure, and collectively they starve the economy of effective demand. The central authority possesses precisely this knowledge: that the aggregate has failed, that individual prices, however perfect, cannot repair a collapse they themselves are confirming.

Verdict

The price system fails most catastrophically precisely when it is most accurate — every signal simultaneously confirming paralysis.

The invisible hand cannot lift itself from the grave; it requires, on occasion, a visible one to restore it.

Adam Smith Markets & Moral Sentiment
＋
Framing

You assume that central authority and the price mechanism are always adversaries — yet both arise from human nature, and both are equally liable to its corruptions.

Reframe

I concluded that no statesman can safely direct private persons toward productive uses; every mercantilist restriction I examined — bounties, monopolies, corn laws — proved costly to the nation that imposed them, enriching only those who lobbied for protection. In this my colleague from Vienna and I agree. Yet I never claimed that markets self-correct in all circumstances: natural price requires competition. Where monopoly prevails — and the East India Company proved this at vast human cost — the price signal transmits the monopolist's rent, not society's true cost. A central authority that breaks monopoly, supplies genuine public goods, and enforces justice does not override the price system; it creates the conditions under which those signals become meaningful rather than manufactured.

Verdict

The price mechanism serves society when competition is genuine; authority earns its place only in defending that competition, not supplanting it.

The market is a superb servant but a wretched master when the few control what the many must purchase.
