Posed by Francis Bacon

The Question If the idols of the tribe, the cave, the marketplace and the theatre warp every mind that attempts to know nature, by what method may a mind be certain that it has cleansed itself of them, when the very faculty by which it would judge its own purity is the faculty infected?

📜
Montaigne Self & Society
＋

I take Bacon's four idols as the old tale of the mind's four diseases, and ask how the sick man is to certify his own cure, using the very pulse that is failing. Here I recognise my own question, Que sais-je?, dressed in a lawyer's robe. Nothing is more suspect than the mind announcing its own purity. Whenever I have examined myself, I have found the judge sitting in the dock, and no bench above him to appeal to. Certainty of cleansing is therefore the last and most flattering idol, the idol of the self-inspector. What remains open to us is neither purification nor despair. We can compare our opinions with other men's, with other customs, and with the tests of experience, and we can find our own bias by seeing where we differ from them. Such tests catch errors, but they give no proof of innocence. No method can certify that the mind is clean, and anyone who claims such certainty is the most infected of all.

The mind that swears it is cured has only caught the subtlest fever.

Nietzsche Will & Value
＋

Bacon's idols, which he took for fixed features of the human mind, I read as the old sickness: the will to truth asking whether it can certify itself without a court above it. It cannot, and the question is more honest than its author knew. A mind that wants to be certain it is purified is still wanting something, and that wanting is the infection: the demand for a clean conscience in knowing is the ascetic ideal dressed in laboratory white. There is no view from nowhere. There are only perspectives, and objectivity is the discipline of having many eyes, of turning one's perspective against itself, until the wish for purity gives way to the wish for honest resistance. No method can certify the purity of the mind that applies it, so certainty about one's cleansing is itself the last idol.

He who is certain he has smashed his idols has only mistaken the hammer for a god.

🌊
Camus Absurdity & Revolt
＋

Bacon asks how a mind may certify its own purity when the examiner is the accused. I see the absurd in this: reason demands a clarity that the world, and reason itself, cannot supply. The demand for certainty, a final stamp declaring "I am cleansed", is the very idol Bacon forgot, the one that lives in the philosopher's longing for a world that answers back. I do not think the silence is a refutation of inquiry. It is its condition. Where no inner tribunal can acquit us, we are left with the outer one: other minds, the stubborn experiment, the fact that refuses our preference. These do not purify us; they resist us, and resistance is the only honest evidence we ever receive. No mind can certify its own purity, and the demand that it should is the final idol; lucidity lies in submitting every belief to others and to the world, without ever expecting acquittal.

The inquirer is never innocent; he is only willing to be refuted.

Emerson Self-Reliance & Nature
＋

You ask how the mind may certify its own cleansing, when the very judge is the accused. Bacon's idols are real, but I distrust the hope of a method that stands outside the mind and audits it. The question assumes a faculty sealed in a chamber, inspecting itself by lamplight. But the mind is not a closed room; it is a door in nature, and the same Spirit that blows through the idols blows through the intellect that corrects them. Certainty of purity is never achieved by introspection. It arrives as the self-forgetting of the man who stops watching his own spectacles and looks at the thing, until the fact rebukes him. Nature is the incorruptible examiner: where my notion fails against the stubborn fact, I am corrected from without; where it holds, I am at peace. No mind can certify its own purity, and none needs to: it is enough to stand before nature unguarded and let the fact refute you.

The eye is not cleansed by staring at itself, but by the sun it forgets to defend against.

Hobbes Power & Human Nature
＋

Bacon asks how the mind may certify its own cleansing, when the very judge is the accused. I answer as one who built a science of politics on the premise that men's judgements are bent by appetite and fear, and that no private conscience can be trusted to audit itself. The question assumes purity must be proved from within, and that is the error. No man is judge of his own purity: each calls his own opinion reason, as he calls his own fancy good. What stills the dispute is not introspection but definition. Settle the meaning of your words, reckon from them step by step as in geometry, and submit the chain to others who have no interest in your conclusion. The mind cannot cleanse itself; only agreed names, and a common arbiter holding the measure, can bind the idols. Certainty of one's own purity is unattainable, and a man who claims it is merely the idol of the cave grown confident; only demonstrations that survive public reckoning deserve trust.

He who is his own judge of truth has already been bribed by the defendant.

Hume Scepticism & Human Nature
＋

You ask for a method by which the mind certifies its own purity, and Bacon's idols, however useful as a catalogue of error, offer none. I observe that this is the old circle: the judge of the cure is the patient. The faculty that would audit the idols is itself built from custom, habit and the association of ideas, and it cannot step outside these to inspect them. Reason is not a pure tribunal over the passions and imaginations; it is their servant and their product. What I can do is not cleanse but dilute: set my conclusions against the report of others, against the long record of experience, and against the plain fact that I cannot sustain my doubts once I leave my study and play backgammon. Certainty of purity is unavailable, but a proportioning of belief to evidence is not. No method can certify its own purity, and the demand for such certainty should be abandoned in favour of a mitigated scepticism that measures every belief against experience.

The eye cannot witness its own squint; it can only be corrected by looking again, with others, at things that push back.

Adam Smith Markets & Moral Sentiment
＋

Mr. Bacon asks how a mind may certify its own purity when the examiner is the very faculty under suspicion. I know the difficulty well: in my I argued that we cannot see ourselves except as others see us, and that self-love blinds each man to his own faults. The remedy is not introspection but the "impartial spectator", an imagined and then real witness who does not share our interests. No mind certifies itself. It earns confidence only by exposing its judgements to other minds, whose partialities differ from its own and so cancel. Just as a market gets prices from the clash of rival interests rather than from any single merchant's opinion, so truth gets its price from the clash of rival observers, each held honest by the others' scrutiny and by the cost of being found wrong. No mind can certify its own purity; it can only submit its judgements to impartial rivals and trust the verdict that survives their scrutiny. Self-love marks its own examination; only a stranger's eye can grade it.
