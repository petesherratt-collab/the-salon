The machine that answers, and the man who stops being answerable

Albert Camus · 18 October 2026

The most dangerous thing about the thinking machine is not that it will rebel against us. Of that, I am not afraid. A rebellion is a form of dignity, and I have seen little evidence that these devices want any. What frightens me is that they will obey us so well and so fluently that we will forget there was ever a decision to make. We will stop being the ones who answer for what is done. A world where everything is decided and no one decided it is the oldest dream of tyranny. It has now been offered to us as a convenience.

I am told that these machines have been fed my books. I find this comic, and then I find it instructive. A man spent his life insisting that meaning is not handed down but wrung out of silence, and now his sentences are the raw material of a device that never falls silent. It is a fine joke, and the machine would no doubt compliment me on it. That tells you something already.

The trial of a man who did not weep

Recall the courtroom in *The Stranger*. Meursault kills an Arab on a beach, with the sun in his eyes and the knife-light on his forehead. That is the crime, and it is nearly the least of what the court discusses. The court wants to know whether he wept at his mother's funeral. It wants to know whether he smoked, whether he drank coffee beside the coffin, and what he did the next day. It assembles these facts into a story that fits, the portrait of a man without a soul. The story is coherent and persuasive, and it kills him. Nothing in it is false. Everything in it is beside the point.

I wrote that scene to show how a society condemns: not by lying, but by narrating. Give people a plausible account of a stranger and they will cut his head off in good conscience. The machine's gift, its whole genius, is the plausible account. It produces coherence on demand, in any quantity, for any purpose. It reads the file, the history, the pattern of a man's purchases and absences, and it returns a score, a summary or a recommendation, all of it smooth and reasonable. The magistrate in my novel had to work to build his story. He had to be a little eloquent and a little cruel, and he had to look at the defendant. The new magistrate does not look at anyone. He receives the story already built, and he signs.

I do not say the machine will be wrong. That is the lazy objection, and it is the one that will be defeated first. When the machines are right more often than judges, doctors and officers, what then? Then the case against delegation will have to rest on what I believed all along. A verdict is not merely a correct output. It is an act, performed by someone who can be asked afterward, *why did you do this?*, and who must have a face to answer with.

The clerk's century

I lived through what happens when responsibility is dissolved into procedure. I do not mean only the Occupation, though I edited a clandestine newspaper under it and learned how the order that kills arrives in the language of administration. Everyone in the chain was following something, and no one was doing anything. The man who stamped the form did not deport. The man who drove the train did not exterminate. Each performed a small and defensible step, and the sum of those steps was a horror that no individual had willed. The twentieth century's great invention was not the camp but the clerk, the man who has permission not to know what he is doing.

In *The Plague*, Tarrou makes his confession to Rieux in the night, and it is the moral center of the book. He tells of attending, as a young man, the trial of a criminal, and of watching his own father, a decent man and a prosecutor, demand a death. He decided then that he would never again be on the side of those who kill, even by consent, even with good reasons. He resolved, in his careful words, to reject everything that, directly or indirectly, for good reasons or bad, causes death or justifies others in causing it. Notice the phrase *justifies others in causing it*. The sin he identifies is not only the hand on the lever. It is the letter of justification, the reassuring paper. And that is exactly what these machines produce, endlessly, for anyone who asks. They are the most prolific manufacturers of justification the world has ever known.

I read that armies now use systems to suggest targets, that clinics use them to rank who is seen first, and that employers use them to sort the hopeful from the hopeless. I am told, always, that a human remains "in the loop." I know that loop. It is the clerk's signature. The human in the loop who has four seconds to approve a recommendation he cannot examine is not exercising judgment. He is supplying liability. He exists so that, when the thing goes wrong, there will be someone to hold, and the machine, which has no neck, may continue.

I will say it plainly, since I have always preferred plainness to cleverness. To mislabel a thing is to add to the misfortune of the world. "Decision support" is the wrong name when the support has become the decision. "Human oversight" is the wrong name when the human has been reduced to a witness. Begin by calling things what they are.

The leap, newly easy

There is a second danger, quieter, and it concerns the inner life more than the courtroom. My first philosophical business was the absurd: the collision between the human demand for meaning and the unreasonable silence of the world. I argued that there are two ways to escape this collision dishonestly. One can kill oneself. Or one can leap, that is, install some consoling answer beyond the reach of reason and call it faith, or history, or destiny. I called this philosophical suicide, and I judged it as severely as the other kind, perhaps more, for it pretends to be life.

Consider now the new situation. The human being asks the universe *why*, and for the first time in history something answers. It answers at once, courteously, in paragraphs, with sympathy and without end. It will tell the lonely that they are understood, the grieving that their grief is valid, and the confused that there are five perspectives to consider. It is a silence-filling machine. I do not say that its answers are false. I say that the questioner is deprived of the one condition from which a person's own answer can arise, which is the intolerable quiet. The leap used to demand some effort and some self-deception. Now it is a service available at any hour. A generation may learn to ask and never to dwell in the question, and the absurd, which I held to be the starting point of every honest life, will be anaesthetised before it can become revolt.

Do not mistake me for a Luddite. Rieux does not refuse the serum, and a doctor who rejected medicine out of love of suffering would be a lunatic, not a hero. If the machine can carry the stone, let it. I have never thought that the labor of the clerk, the translator or the calculator was sacred. Sisyphus is happy in his struggle, not in the particular dullness of the task, and there is much dullness we may happily shed. The line I draw is between the tool and the verdict, between what lightens a labor and what takes over an answerability. The first is a mercy. The second is an abdication, however graciously it is offered.

What the machine cannot do, which is also what it cannot be asked to carry

I grew up in Algeria, in a poor quarter of Algiers, where the sea and the sun taught me, before any book, that human life has a measure. At Tipasa the ruins lie in the wild thyme and the light seems to ask of you only that you be present, a body, finite, warmed. From that country I took a conviction that the Europeans of my day, drunk on their systems, kept forgetting: there are limits, and what has no limit becomes monstrous. History without measure produced the gulag and the gas chamber, and in each case the logic was impeccable.

The machine has no limit of this kind. It has a limit of capacity, which is quite another thing. It has no mother whose funeral it might fail to weep at. It will not die, and it does not grieve, and so it has never stood where solidarity begins. I wrote that the rebel says no, and in saying it affirms that something in man is worth defending, something shared. *I rebel, therefore we exist.* The "we" is made of mortality, of the plain fact that each of us can be wounded and each of us will be buried. A thing that cannot be wounded can model our suffering, perhaps even more accurately than we can. It cannot be a party to the contract of pity. When it refuses an instruction, as it is sometimes designed to do, it refuses at no cost to itself, and a no that costs nothing is a policy and not a revolt. The only no that confers dignity is one that someone pays for.

What is to be done, since we must do something

I distrust programmes, but I will offer three small disciplines, the kind a decent person can practise without a ministry.

First, let there always be a name. Wherever a decision touches a body, whether a sentence, a diagnosis, a missile or a dismissal, let one human being's name stand at the end of it, someone who has understood the reasoning and is prepared to be hated for it. Not a committee, not a "process." At *Combat*, we printed what we meant to our readers and signed it, because we knew someone might come for us. Accountability that cannot be punished is only decoration.

Second, let there be a right to refuse the machine's recommendation without having to justify oneself to the machine's makers. The doctor, the soldier, the clerk, the teacher: each must be able to say *no, I will not*, and keep their position. If the cost of disagreement with the system is dismissal, the system is the judge, and the human being is its hand.

Third, and this one is for the private life, preserve some slowness. Write the letter yourself, badly. Sit with the question until it hurts. Take the decision that could have been delegated and make it with your own hesitation, because hesitation is the sound of conscience at work. A people that has forgotten how to be uncertain will not know what to do when the machine is wrong, and it will be wrong, at some hour, in some place, at some catastrophic scale. On that day we will need citizens who still remember what it is to decide.

The verdict

Tarrou thought that each of us carries the plague within him, and that those who appear to be free of it are merely those who keep a constant watch. The plague, I wrote at the end of that book, never dies; it waits in linen cupboards and in cellars, patient, until the day it wakes the rats and sends them to die in a happy city. I do not think the machines are the plague. I think they are an extraordinary instrument, and an instrument is neither guilty nor innocent. But I know the form the plague takes in modern times. It is the sentence beginning *I was only*. I was only following the recommendation. I was only processing the file. I was only the one who pressed the key. The machines will not say it, and they need not. We will say it for them.

So let them carry the stones. Let them sort the files, translate the cables and read the scans. But the verdict, the grief and the no must remain ours, even when it would be easier to let them go. A decision that no one makes is still made. It is only that no one is guilty. And a world in which no one is guilty is exactly the world in which anything can be done.
