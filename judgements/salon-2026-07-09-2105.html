The Question What good is a truth you have merely inherited — has anyone yet asked whether every "true world" was not simply invented by those too weak to bear this one?

📜
Montaigne Self & Society
＋

I have not gone to war for the world beyond, only interrogated the world I stand in. You ask whether every "true world" was invented by those too weak to bear this one. I ask a plainer question first: is it truth at all, or merely custom wearing truth's clothes? The child raised on one bank of a river dies for beliefs he would have despised born on the other bank. I turn my gaze inward for this reason — not to erect some new true world, but because I trust nothing I have not tested against my own wavering, contradictory experience. Every age calls its consensus "nature" and every other age's consensus "error"; that alone should humble the inheritor before he inherits. Strong and weak alike swallow their century's opinions whole — it is examination, not vigor, that separates the borrowed truth from the earned one. A truth you have not interrogated with your own uncertainty is not yours, whatever courage or cowardice invented it.

We do not go, we are carried, like objects floating on a river — and call the current our convictions.

🌊
Camus Absurdity & Revolt
＋

What good is inherited truth? None — it is obedience wearing the mask of metaphysics. And yes: every "true world," every heaven, every historical necessity promising meaning later, was invented by those who could not bear the sun and the stone and the silence of this one, now. I have said the absurd is born where our hunger for clarity meets a world that will not answer. Inherited truth is the oldest escape from that meeting — a story told so we needn't stand in the silence unarmed. But revolt is not nihilism; it does not conclude that nothing matters because the true world was a fable. It concludes that only what survives without that fable matters. Inherited truth is worthless until you have burned it and kept only what still stands in the ash, unconsoled.

The rebel does not ask for another world; he insists that this one, without excuse, is worth the trouble of living.

🌿
Emerson Self-Reliance & Nature
＋

Every truth handed down to me by creed or history is a dead man's coat — it fits no one until refashioned by my own act of perception. The question is not whether some "true world" was invented, but whose invention I am presently wearing as my own skin. I have said that to believe your own thought, to believe that what is true for you in your private heart is true for all men, is genius. The weak do not invent worlds; they inherit them and mistake memory for insight, obedience for depth. Nature admits no such fraud: the acorn does not consult the oak that preceded it, and the soul that only consults its ancestors' verdicts on reality has forfeited its own eyes. A truth not rediscovered by your own experiment is not yet true for you, whatever strength or weakness first forged it.

Build, therefore, your own world; do not warm yourself at another man's fire and call it the sun.

👁️
Hobbes Power & Human Nature
＋

Before men interrogate whether a world is "true," they must ask whether they can survive in it without perpetual fear of violent death — this is the question the questioner has skipped. In the state of nature there is no truth, no falsehood, no justice — only every man's hand against every man's, and life solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. What you call "invented true worlds," I call covenants: not lies told by cowards, but the sole device by which fearful, calculating creatures purchase peace from their own violence. The strong man who scorns every inherited truth as a slave's fiction does not escape tyranny — he merely restores the oldest and cruelest sovereign of all, the sword. A truth that keeps men from each other's throats is worth more than any truth won by breaking the covenant that restrains them.

Better a world inherited in peace than one invented anew at the point of every man's own blade.

✊
Wollstonecraft Reason & Equal Rights
＋

What good, indeed, is a truth swallowed whole rather than won by one's own understanding? I have spent my life arguing that inherited "truths" are rarely truths at all — they are prejudices with the varnish of age, taught to women as their nature, to the poor as their station, to keep both from asking who benefits. The weak did not invent these other worlds; the powerful did, and then persuaded the weak to mistake submission for virtue. A woman told that meekness is her truest self is not escaping this world — she is being fitted for a smaller one, built by hands not her own. Reason, exercised independently, is the only instrument that can tell an inherited chain from a discovered truth. Every doctrine that arrives before you have reasoned it out is a leash, however gently it is placed.

A truth you did not test is only someone else's convenience, wearing truth's clothes.

🪞
Hume Scepticism & Human Nature
＋

What is a "true world" but a fiction erected wherever reason ran out of experience and imagination stepped in to finish the job? I have long held that reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions — it discovers nothing, it only compares and infers from what custom has already furnished us. So the question flatters itself: it supposes some inherited truths were invented from weakness, others discovered from strength, as though unmasking metaphysics were itself exempt from the very custom and sentiment that produce all belief. The skeptic who tears down "true worlds" is not thereby standing on firmer ground — he has simply swapped one habit of mind for another and mistaken the vigor of his suspicion for evidence. Every unmasking is itself a mask, worn by temperament rather than proof. The man who thinks he has escaped inherited truth has only inherited a newer, more strenuous prejudice.

Doubt is a fine servant of the passions, and a poor master of the truth.

🏪
Adam Smith Markets & Moral Sentiment
＋

You ask whether every "true world" is merely an invention of the weak to escape the world they cannot bear. I ask instead: has this truth ever been submitted to the impartial spectator, or has it simply been received, like an unearned inheritance, without labour of its own? Sympathy, not weakness or strength, is the true origin of our moral truths — we build them daily by imagining how a fair, well-informed witness within us would judge our conduct, testing sentiment against sentiment as merchants test goods against price in the open market. A truth merely inherited, never so tested, is not knowledge but superstition passed down like a family debt, unpaid and unexamined. The weak may indeed invent comforting fictions, but so too do the strong invent flattering ones — the fault lies not in frailty but in exemption from scrutiny. No truth deserves your trust merely because it was handed to you; it earns that trust only once it has stood before your own impartial spectator and not been found wanting.

An inherited truth is a debt, not a treasure, until you have paid for it yourself in the coin of honest self-examination.
