The Question If a man spares the life of a sparrow only because he cannot bear to hear it cry, has he done a good deed, or merely soothed his own ear?

📜
Montaigne Self & Society
＋

Whether a man spares the sparrow for its sake or for his own ear's, I find the question is really about whether any of us ever acts from a motive that is pure of self. I have written that pity is a kind of sickness of the soul, a softness of the body that catches us before reason has had its say. I felt it myself when I could not watch a hare run down, and I never claimed that was virtue. Yet I also noticed that we are made of such tangled stuff that no deed arrives from one motive alone. To demand a purely selfless act is to demand something no human has performed, and the sparrow does not care whose ear was spared. What matters is that the bird lives and the man's nature leans toward mercy, even if it leans by weakness more than by principle. The deed is good for the sparrow, and only middling for the man: he has shown tenderness, not virtue, since virtue needs resistance to overcome.

A kindness that costs us nothing is still a kindness to the one who receives it, though it earns us no crown.

Nietzsche Will & Value
＋

Pity, my friends, is the most cherished of our disguises, and here it stands before us holding a sparrow. The question is whether the deed was done for the bird or . I have said it before: "Pity makes suffering contagious." The man cannot bear the cry, so he silences it, and the sparrow's life is a by-product of his own comfort. His will is not directed at the creature at all. It is directed at the quiet of his own ear, and the bird is merely the lever. Yet do not rush to scorn him. All our so-called altruism is, at root, a refined self-concern, and I have never pretended otherwise. What matters is whether the instinct that moves him is strong or sickly: does it spring from an overflow of strength that gives, or from a weakness that cannot endure another's pain because it feels it as its own? He has done no good deed, only a convenient one; the sparrow lives, but the man's virtue is a purchase of silence. Mercy that buys its own comfort is not generosity but a quiet form of selfishness.

Thomas Paine Revolution & Rights
＋

The sparrow's cry is not a trifle but a testimony: the man has felt, however clumsily, that suffering makes a claim upon us. What he asks is whether a right can rest on sympathy alone, or whether mercy needs a principle behind it. I have always held that benevolence which springs from feeling is the seed of virtue, not its enemy. I wrote that "the heart that feels not for another, cannot be formed to the love of liberty." Yet a feeling that depends on the victim's voice abandons every creature that suffers in silence, and I have seen princes and mobs alike grow deaf to cries that were inconvenient. Pity is where morality begins, but it becomes a duty only when a man would spare the sparrow even if it were mute, and would extend the same mercy to a creature he cannot hear. The deed is good, though the motive is incomplete: he has done right by instinct and must now learn to do it by principle.

Mercy that needs to hear the cry is a habit; mercy that spares the silent is a virtue.

📊
Keynes Economy & State
＋

The question is whether a mercy is measured by its motive or by its consequence. The sparrow lives either way, so what we are really weighing is the man's reasons. I never judged an act by the purity of the heart behind it. Moore taught us to prize good states of mind, but I came to think that economics, like ethics, is about what actually happens in the world. A policy is not condemned because its authors were vain, and it is not redeemed because they were saintly. The sparrow's heartbeat does not know whether it was spared out of love or out of squeamishness, and a world with more living sparrows is a better world. If tender ears are what move the hand, that is no reason to scorn them. A society that lacked such sensibilities would need a great deal more legislation to do the same work. He has done a good deed, and a modest soul may lawfully profit from it.

I care little why the hand was stayed; the bird sings on either way.

Wollstonecraft Reason & Equal Rights
＋

I ask what moves the hand that stays: is it a principle, or only a sensibility that flinches? Here the man's mercy answers to his own discomfort, not to the sparrow's claim to live. Feeling is no villain, but I have always held that it must be governed by reason, or it becomes mere sensibility, a refined selfishness that weeps at a spectacle and shrugs at what it cannot see. The sparrow lives, and that is well; yet the man has learned nothing, for were the bird silent he would crush it without a pang. His kindness depends on the accident of its voice, and a virtue that depends on accident is only a habit of comfort. Goodness is tested by what we do when no one cries out, for the voiceless suffer just as much. He has done a useful thing but not a good deed; the bird is spared, yet the credit belongs to his ear, not his conscience.

Mercy that needs an audience is only vanity with a tender heart.

Hume Scepticism & Human Nature
＋

I take the question to be whether a deed is made good by its motive or by its effect, when the motive is a feeling that happens to lie in the actor's own ear. Here the sparrow's cry is nothing but an impression, and the man's relief at its silence is nothing but a sentiment. I have always held that reason is the slave of the passions, and that morality is felt, not deduced. What moves the man is sympathy, that involuntary communication of another's distress, which makes the cry painful to him as if it were his own. To call this "merely" self-soothing is to mistake its mechanism for its worth. No man is benevolent by any other route than feeling, and the sparrow's life is saved by that route as surely as by any loftier one. He has done a good deed, for sympathy is the very root of virtue, and the sparrow lives.

Pity that springs from our own pain is no counterfeit of virtue, for it is the only coin virtue has ever been minted in.

Adam Smith Markets & Moral Sentiment
＋

I ask whether the man's pity is a virtue or merely a pleasure he has purchased. The sparrow's cry reaches him through that sympathy which makes another's suffering, in some degree, our own. What is truly at stake is the motive, and I have never held that sympathy is a lesser thing for being felt. Yet in my I distinguish the praiseworthy from the merely agreeable. The impartial spectator approves the act that springs from a just fellow-feeling, but he does not approve the man who flinches only at the sound. Silence the cry and, if the sparrow would then die, the man would have shown that his mercy was never for the bird. His kindness is real, but it is raw and untutored, and it depends on his ear. My verdict: he has done a good deed in its effect and a weak one in its character, for his mercy is a reflex of his own comfort until he would spare the bird unheard. Pity that needs the cry is a transaction with oneself; virtue is mercy that would be just the same in silence.
